The Off Season

A fence in January

Most Toledo fence companies stop taking work in November and start again in April. We do not, and it is not bravado — in the clay most of this area sits on, winter is a genuinely good time to build. Here is the honest version, including where it is not.

Why winter stops most contractors

It is not the cold. It is the concrete.

A fence set the conventional way needs a poured footing at every post, and concrete cures much more slowly as the temperature drops. Below about 40°F it needs warmed water, a cold-weather mix, or insulating blankets over every hole, and you do not pour onto frozen ground at all — frozen soil cannot take up the moisture, so the footing cures unevenly and ends up weaker than the one you paid for. General guidance is to avoid pouring if the temperature will fall below freezing within 24 hours, which in a Toledo January rules out most days.

Add the digging. Frozen clay behaves like rock, so holes need a carbide auger, a breaker, or ground-thawing blankets left out overnight. That is slower, more expensive and much harder on a lawn. Faced with all that, closing for four months is a reasonable business decision.

Why it does not stop us

In clay we do not pour footings. We drive an 8-foot galvanized steel post four feet into undisturbed ground — a foot below the 36-inch frost line Lucas County sets — and there is no concrete anywhere in the job.

Take the concrete out and every winter objection goes with it. Nothing has to cure, so temperature stops mattering. Nothing has to be kept above freezing overnight. There is no waiting between setting posts and hanging panels, because a driven post is load-bearing the moment it is in. And there are no open holes sitting in your yard between visits.

Frozen ground is also not the enemy people assume. A driven post gets its holding from soil closing tightly around its full length, and firm ground does that well. What we are driving through is the top crust; below the frost line the clay is unfrozen and behaves exactly as it does in July.

What you actually get out of it

  • No queue. In June you are behind everyone who called in April. In January you are not behind anyone.
  • Less damage to your yard. Dormant, firm turf takes machinery far better than soft June grass. Spring is the worst month for ruts, not winter.
  • The fence is up before you want it. Pools open in May, dogs need containing the first warm weekend, and graduation parties are booked in March. A fence finished in February is a fence you never think about again.
  • Prices are what they are. Material costs do not fall in winter and we do not pretend otherwise — what changes is availability, not the number.

Where winter genuinely is not the answer

If your lot is on sand — the Oak Openings ground southwest of Toledo, or the strip along the lake — the right build there is a concrete-set 4×6, and every problem in the first section applies to it. We can do it with warm mix and blankets, but it is fussier work and we will tell you plainly if waiting is the better call for your yard. Why the soil decides which one you get.

Two other honest limits. Deep snow has to come off the line before we can set it out accurately, and if it is falling faster than we can clear it we will move the day. And vinyl gets brittle in hard cold — it goes up fine, but it wants careful handling, so a bitter week may be a cedar week rather than a vinyl one.

We are also not going to work a day that produces a worse fence. If conditions are wrong we move you, we do not push through and hand you something that has to be fixed in April.

From Nick

The call I get most often in March is somebody who wanted the fence up before their pool opened and has just found out the earliest date anyone can offer is July. They could have had it in January. Nobody told them that was possible, because most people they rang had already shut for the season and had no reason to mention it.
